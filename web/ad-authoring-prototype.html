New to audio description? A short primer

What AD is: a narration track that says what the picture alone tells sighted viewers — actions, places, expressions, and words that appear on screen — so blind and low-vision viewers get the same story, not a lesser one.

Standard vs. < Standard Gap: standard AD squeezes the description into a natural pause in the dialogue. A standard gap here is 3 seconds — about 7 words at narration speed. When the only pauses are shorter than that — < Standard Gap — the line can't fit without talking over someone, so playback pauses while it is read. Those are the spots labeled < Standard Gap.

Why this episode needs so many points: the lesson is on the screen. The word cards — year, yard, my, gym, type — appear while everyone keeps talking, so a listener who can't see them misses the teaching itself. Scene changes, entrances, and jokes told in pictures count too.

Why the first draft felt thin: it marked only the biggest beats — 22 spots. This pass adds the Sound Festival acts and the smaller fence beats: 33 spots. The rule for adding more: if a sighted viewer would notice it and a listener would miss it, it earns a spot. If the dialogue already says it, it doesn't — repeating the talk is just noise.

House rules for the text: present tense; describe, don't interpret; read on-screen words exactly as written; never talk over dialogue or song lyrics inside a standard gap; budget about 2.5 words per second of window. Every suggestion here is a draft — check it against the picture before you accept it.

Full test plan — a public-domain run-through

The film: Night of the Living Dead (1968) is public domain — its copyright notice was left off the original release — and it is an ideal test: clear dialogue gaps, long quiet stretches, and a story told in pictures. Charade (1963) and The Little Shop of Horrors (1960) are public domain too. All three are free to download from the Internet Archive (archive.org).

  1. Add it: use + Add clip in the top row, then either press Generate spots in the spots panel — it reads the audio right in your browser, finds the quiet gaps, and drops in pause spots (you write the descriptions; there is no transcript) — or, with the local scan server running, press Send for full scan for the full treatment: transcript, pauses, scene changes, drafted lines if you picked a drafter, and a captions track.
  2. Eyes-closed pass: play it with the screen covered and note every moment you feel lost. Those timestamps are your must-describe list.
  3. Mark: put a spot on every scene change, every piece of on-screen text, and every silent action the plot depends on. Coverage formula: spots ≥ scene changes + on-screen texts + silent plot beats.
  4. Budget the words: window seconds × 2.5 = the most words a standard gap holds. Anything longer needs a < Standard Gap spot, where playback pauses.
  5. Set the edges: zoom in, then drag or nudge In/Out onto the exact moment. A yellow flash means an edge hit its limit — spots never overlap — and Undo, beside Out, takes back the last move.
  6. Review pass: turn on Play to next spot, step through every spot, use Hear the line to hear each description at its moment, and edit or accept each one.
  7. Export and listen back: download the AD script (WebVTT) from the spots panel — and, separately, the captions download beside it: a different track for a different audience. For the two built-in clips it downloads real cues made from their transcripts (Clip 2: 28 cues; Clip 3: 307 cues); a clip you added gets captions the same way once a full scan brings its transcript back — unscanned, its captions button explains that instead of downloading. Then listen to the film audio-only with the descriptions read at their spots. If the story survives with no picture, the AD works.
  8. Start over any time: the × on a clip tab removes it, so you can wipe a test and begin a clean project whenever you like.

Where this stands: you picked Option 1 — a small program on your own computer — and version 1 is built and tested in its own project area (the server folder): transcription, pauses, scene changes, and drafted lines when a provider key is set. On the test clips it reproduced Clip 2 exactly (28 transcript segments, 2 pause spots, about 13 seconds) and scanned the full Letter People episode in about 1 minute 45 seconds (299 segments, 46 spots — 4 pause, 42 scene). On noisy vintage audio the exact gaps vary a little from run to run — the transcription hears it slightly differently each time — which is what the draggable markers are for. This page is now wired to it: a clip you add gets a Send for full scan button in the spots panel — pick a drafter (or “No drafter — timings only”), press it, and the spots, the waveform colors, and a captions track made from the transcript load back into the clip, ready to review. The send only works while the server is running on your machine (start it with start.sh in the server folder); if it can't be reached, the button says so and nothing is faked or sent anywhere else. One known snag: the server must also grant this page permission to read its replies (the browser's CORS headers) — if a send reports it was blocked even though the server is running, the server needs that small update; Generate spots and the chat flow keep working either way.

Phase 2 — the audio exports are the second half of this same step: the other two audio shapes are not separate projects. They are designed as one more endpoint on the same server, POST /export, with three modes — ad-only (just the spoken descriptions as one audio file), second-track (that voice packaged into the video as a switchable second audio track), and mixed (the video's audio replaced by the ducked mix). A speech step comes first: a synthetic voice reads each line of the AD script — the WebVTT is the master it reads from. Phase 2 is designed and begun, and the export menu in the spots panel already lists all three shapes; the endpoint itself is not built yet, so picking one still explains instead of downloading.

How it works: the scan server runs on your own computer, alongside this page, and the page would talk to it at a local address on the same machine — your footage never leaves your computer. The pipeline is the same one a manual scan uses: 1. pull the audio out of the video, 2. transcribe the dialogue with speech-to-text (a Whisper-class model), 3. find the pauses and the scene changes, 4. draft a description line for each spot, and 5. send the finished spot list back — this page loads it exactly like one of the built-in clips.

Where FFmpeg fits: FFmpeg is the free, standard tool for taking media files apart and putting them back together — it is a workhorse program, not a service, and it runs entirely on your machine. In this project it has three jobs: extracting the audio from a video so the scan has something to transcribe; later, muxing — packaging a finished AD voice track into the video file as a second, switchable audio track; and mixing — producing the version where the AD voice is baked into the audio with the original sound ducked underneath. The scan (step 1) needs only the first job; the audio exports are phase 2 of the same step, and they are FFmpeg jobs on the server for the same reason the scan is: this page can't do them.

Packaging, plainly: the server is one small local program you install once and start when you want to scan — think of it as a helper app that sits next to this page. It carries its own tools with it (the speech-to-text model and FFmpeg), listens only on your own machine, and this page talks to it the way it would talk to any local address. Nothing to sign up for, nothing uploaded. If the tool is ever sold, that same program is what ships in the box.

The AI is a plug, not a fixture: transcription, pauses, and scene detection are plain local code — they need no AI at all. Only the drafting step uses one, so the server has a single setting: “Draft descriptions with: Kavi / Claude / another AI service / a local model / none — timings only.” Kavi drafting is not available from the server (there is no Muse API for it) — drafting through Kavi stays the chat flow, where you send the file here. Claude or OpenAI draft through an API key you set on the server once (an environment variable when you start it) — the spots panel's drafter picker simply tells the server which one to use. Pick “none” and you get exactly what Generate spots gives you today: timings, no text. Someone with no access to Kavi loses nothing but one option in that menu — same spots, same format, same page.

What doesn't change: writing your own lines at the suggested intervals stays exactly as valuable as it is now, and pushing the automatic side further doesn't touch it. The server only produces drafts; every line still lands here for you to edit, accept, or replace — and an AD line must never just repeat what the dialogue already says.

What is a WebVTT? Captions vs. audio description — and the audio exports

WebVTT (Web Video Text Tracks) is a plain-text file of timed cues — the same format family as subtitle files. Each cue is a start time, an end time, and the words:

00:00.000 --> 00:09.500 Colorful cartoon letters and Letter People characters parade across a bright yellow background.

How a text file helps audio description: the WebVTT is the master timed-text script — the single source every other output is made from. The AD audio track is rendered from it: a voice (synthetic or recorded) reads each cue at its time, and the original sound is ducked underneath. Fix a line or a timing in the script and every track made from it is fixed. It is not a competing output next to the audio — it is the thing the audio is built out of. It is also the file players and editors already understand, which is why it is the right master format.

Two tracks, two audiences — never mixed: the same format carries two completely different kinds of track, and keeping them apart is the whole point.

  • Captions write down the dialogue and important sounds — what is said, who says it, [music], [laughter]. They are for viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing: people who can see the picture but can't hear the words.
  • Audio description writes down the picture — actions, places, expressions, words that appear on screen. It is for viewers who are blind or low-vision: people who can hear the words but can't see what is happening.

Opposite audiences, opposite content. A caption track must never contain descriptions, and a description track must never contain dialogue — and an AD line must never describe what the audio or the captions already say, because the listener is already hearing it. That is why the spots panel offers two clearly separate downloads, labeled by audience: the AD script (your edited spots — works today) and the captions track (dialogue — for the two built-in clips it downloads real cues made from their transcripts; see its status below).

The three audio shapes all start from the AD script:

  • AD audio track only — one audio file containing just the spoken descriptions, silent everywhere else, timed to the spots. Small and portable; a player or editor lays it over the video.
  • Video with a second audio track — the AD track embedded inside the video file alongside the original sound. One file to share; viewers switch the description on or off in their player. This is how described video is usually distributed.
  • Video with the audio replaced — one mixed track: the original sound, ducked under each spoken description, baked in. Plays correctly everywhere with no settings to find — but the untouched original audio is gone from that copy.

What this page can do today: the AD WebVTT script — that download works, with your edited times and text exactly as they stand. All three audio shapes are phase 2 of the local scan server — the step now begun, its /export endpoint designed but not built yet: a voice has to read every line, and then FFmpeg (running on the server, on your machine) mixes or muxes it into a file. The export menu lists all three so the choice is visible; picking one today explains this instead of pretending to download one. Captions status: the two built-in clips now carry real caption cues made from their original Whisper transcripts — Clip 2 has 28 cues and Clip 3 has 307 — and their captions downloads work. Two honest limits: the transcripts hold segment timings only, no word-by-word timings, and Clip 3's boundaries are whole- or half-second coarse (about ±0.5s), so a cue can start or end a fraction early or late. The words are the transcript's words, kept faithful — including where Whisper misheard the puppets (it heard “gym” as “Jim”, “my” as “mine”, and “Miss I” as “his eye”); Clip 2 has one flagged line of the same kind. Those garbles are not silently fixed or hidden — correcting them is a human listen-and-fix pass, and this prototype has no caption editor yet. Sound labels were used only where the transcript supports them (the theme songs) — no laughter labels were invented. A clip you add yourself has no cues until it is scanned: press Send for full scan with the local server running and the returning transcript becomes its captions track; until then its captions button honestly explains there is nothing to download.

Saving your work — the progress file (no autosave, on purpose)

No autosave — by design: nothing on this page saves itself. You save your work yourself, when you choose to, with the button below. That is deliberate: a file you saved on purpose is a checkpoint you control; an automatic save can quietly overwrite the last good state — the soft-lock problem. If you don't press Save, closing the page loses the edits, exactly as it should until you say otherwise.

The risk: this page keeps your edits — spot times, descriptions, review state, painted colors — only in this tab's memory. Close the page and they are gone; that is the nature of a static page with no account and no server.

The fix, in the spots panel: Save progress downloads a small file (ad-progress.json) holding every clip's spots, text, review state, painted waveform colors, and your custom palette. Restore progress loads that file back and puts everything onto the clips again. Save early, save often — keep the file somewhere safe, and re-save as the work grows.

Its one limit: a save file can't hold a video. The two built-in clips are always here, so their edits always restore. A clip you added yourself exists only in this session — its edits restore only if you restore during that same session, before the page is closed.
